The real price tag

By 16 March, Australians had used up a whole year’s share of the planet. So what are we buying, what do we say we care about, and where does it all end up?

By Oliver Moses, October 2026

1 We run out by March

Every year, nature regrows a certain amount of forest, fish and crops, and soaks up a certain amount of carbon pollution. A country’s Overshoot Day is the date the world would use all of that up if everyone lived like that country’s people. The earlier the date, the bigger the footprint.

Australia’s Overshoot Day in 2026 was 16 March, the 10th earliest of 82 countries. Only nine countries ran out sooner, mostly wealthy, high-consuming ones such as Qatar, Canada and the United States. Most of the world lasts until mid-year or later.

2 We care, but price comes first

A big footprint starts with what goes in the trolley. When Australians choose their food, almost half say price, specials or loyalty points matter most. Only 12% put the environment first.

We are more focused on price than shoppers in other countries (48% compared with 37%), and it is easy to see why: three in four Australians say the cost of living is the biggest threat they face this year.

That doesn’t mean Australians don’t care. Among those worried about climate change, almost everyone has changed how they shop. But the most popular changes, buying only what they need and wasting less, also save money.

Fewer than one in four buy food with a green certification, an option that often costs more.

When Australians do look for something sustainable, they look for “made in Australia” far more than shoppers overseas. They care less about pesticides, packaging and fair trade labels.

And only 35% of Australians would pay more to look after the land, compared with 44% of shoppers worldwide.

3 Green labels we can’t always trust

For shoppers who do want to buy green, labels are meant to help. But in 2022, the consumer watchdog (the ACCC) checked the websites of 247 businesses and found that 57% made green claims it was concerned about.

Many used vague words like “eco-friendly” with no proof, set green goals with no plan, or showed logos made to look like official certifications. The worst sectors were everyday buys: cosmetics, clothes and food.

Even a trusted label can mislead without context. Australia has more certified organic farmland than any other country: 53 million hectares, more than half the world’s total.

But most of it is outback cattle country. Only 15% of Australia’s farmland is organic, behind countries such as Austria (27%) and Italy (19%), which have far less farmland but more of it organic.

4 Our old clothes don’t disappear

Clothing shows what happens after we buy. The average Australian buys 56 new items of clothing a year, and as a country we get rid of almost 400,000 tonnes of it. Just over half goes straight in the bin.

The rest goes to charity, but op shops can only sell about a quarter of what they are given. More than half of all donated clothing is packed up and shipped overseas.

In 2025, Australia shipped 123,500 tonnes of worn clothing overseas. Nine in every ten tonnes went to just three countries: the United Arab Emirates, Pakistan and Malaysia.

The United Arab Emirates is a sorting hub that sells much of it on again. A lot of it ends up in countries without the managed landfills that Australia has.

5 What we recycle depends on where we live

What stays in Australia is recycled unevenly. Nationally, 64% of our waste was recycled in 2024–25. South Australia, which started the country’s first bottle and can refund scheme in 1977, led with 73%. The Northern Territory recycled just 37%, though that is more than double its rate ten years ago.

The material matters even more than the state. Metal and building rubble are mostly recycled everywhere. Plastic mostly isn’t. And the clothes from the last chapter are almost never recycled: outside South Australia, no state recycles more than 7% of its textiles.

6 The second-hand comeback

There is good news. More Australians are buying clothes that already exist. In 2016, almost no one searched for Depop, an app for buying and selling second-hand clothes. Ten years later, it is searched about as often as charity shops.

Charity shops are more popular too. Searches dropped sharply in April 2020, when COVID-19 lockdowns closed op shops, then bounced back and kept climbing. They have roughly doubled since 2019.

The real price tag

The price on the shelf leaves a lot out. It doesn’t show the planet’s resources we use up by March, the green claims we can’t check, or the clothes that end up in landfill here and overseas. Those costs are real, even if we never see them at the checkout.

The good news is that many of the habits that help cost nothing extra: buy less, waste less, question vague green claims, and give clothes a second life. If everyone lived like us, the planet’s budget for 2026 would have run out on 16 March. What we put in our trolleys helps decide how early it runs out next year.
