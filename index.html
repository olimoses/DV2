The real price tag

What Australians buy, what we say we care about, and where our stuff ends up.

By Oliver Moses, October 2026

1 We spend the year’s budget early

A country’s Overshoot Day is the date by which humanity would use up everything nature can renew in a year, if everyone lived like that country’s people. The earlier the date, the heavier the footprint.

In 2026, Australia reached its Overshoot Day on 16 March, the 10th earliest of the 82 countries on the list. The nine countries that got there sooner include Qatar, Canada and the United States.

2 At the checkout, price wins

Asked what matters most when choosing food, almost half of Australians said price, promotions or loyalty rewards. Only 12% put the environment first.

Caring about the planet still shows up at the checkout, just in cheaper ways. Among Australians worried about climate change, the most common changes are buying only what they need and wasting less, habits that also save money. Paying for food with a sustainability certification is far less common.

And when Australians do look for something sustainable, they look for “made in Australia” much more than shoppers in other countries, and care less about pesticides, packaging and fair trade.

The same pattern shows up when people are asked to pay extra. Only 35% of Australians would pay more to support the health of the land, compared with 44% of shoppers globally. Which raises a question: when Australians do pay for a green label, can they trust it?

3 Can you trust the label?

In October 2022, the consumer watchdog, the ACCC, checked the websites of 247 businesses across eight sectors. More than half made environmental claims that raised concerns: vague words like “eco-friendly” with nothing to back them up, green goals with no plan, and logos designed to look like official certifications.

The three worst sectors are things most of us buy every week: cosmetics, clothes and food.

Independent certification is meant to cut through the noise, and “certified organic” is one of the most familiar labels. Australia has more certified organic farmland than any other country by a huge margin. But look at the colours: as a share of all its farmland, Australia (15%) trails Austria (27%), Uruguay (25%) and Italy (19%).

4 Where our old clothes go

Australians buy around 56 new items of clothing each, every year. We also get rid of almost 400,000 tonnes of clothing a year, and just over half of it goes straight in the bin.

The rest is donated to charity. But op shops can only resell about a quarter of what they receive. More than half of all donated clothing is packed up and shipped overseas.

So where does it go? The latest trade figures show Australia shipped 123,500 tonnes of worn clothing overseas in 2025. More than half went to the United Arab Emirates, a sorting hub that sells clothing on to other countries. Much of it ends up in places that lack the managed landfills Australia relies on.

5 What we recycle at home

Australia recycled 64% of its waste in 2024–25, but how much depends on where you live. South Australia, home of the country’s first container deposit scheme in 1977, recycled 73%. The Northern Territory recycled just over a third. Most states have improved a lot over the past decade.

Break it down by material and a clear pattern appears. Metals and building rubble are mostly recycled everywhere. Plastics rarely are. And textiles, including the clothes from the last chapter, almost never are: outside South Australia, no state recycles more than 7%.

6 The second-hand shift

There are signs of change. In 2016, hardly anyone in Australia searched Google for Depop, an app for buying and selling second-hand clothes. A decade later, it is searched about as often as charity shops, and interest in charity shops has itself roughly doubled since 2019.

So, what’s the real price tag?

Australians use up a year’s worth of the planet’s resources by mid-March. At the checkout, price still wins, and the green labels meant to guide us are often vague or unproven. Much of what we donate ends up overseas, and almost none of our textile waste is recycled at home.

But the habits that help most are often the cheapest ones: buying only what we need, wasting less, and buying second-hand. More and more Australians are already doing all three.
